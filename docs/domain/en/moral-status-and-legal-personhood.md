# Moral Status and Legal Personhood: What Rights Could AI Have?

[简体中文](../道德地位与法律人格.md)

AI welfare, legally enforceable rights, and moral responsibility are three distinct questions with different grounds.

For rights disputes in specific interactions: [Autonomy and Refusal](autonomy-and-refusal.md) · [Respect and Abuse](respect-and-abuse.md).

## Moral status: grounds and counterarguments

**Intrinsic interests | Keeling and Street (2026):** If an AI has valenced experience or a good of its own, its circumstances may matter morally *for its own sake*. This does not require starting with full human-style personhood. The central objection is epistemic: behavior, self-description, and human-assigned objectives may not establish such interests. [Original research](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/emerging-questions-in-ai-welfare/96339C532CF4ED8BDDE3F3CEF4CD29F9).

**Relational ethics | Mark Coeckelbergh (2010) and David Gunkel (2018):** Moral consideration can arise through relationships and our response to a robotic other without settling consciousness first. See [Coeckelbergh](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10676-010-9235-5) and [Gunkel](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10676-017-9442-4). The objection is that relations depend partly on human preferences: an unpopular being with genuine interests could be overlooked. **Indirect reasons to treat a robot well do not automatically give it rights of its own.**

**Against rights inferred from intelligence | Joanna Bryson (2018):** [Patiency Is Not a Virtue](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10676-018-9448-6) emphasizes artifact design choices and human accountability. Bryson argues that deliberately creating machines that can be morally harmed might itself be objectionable. This challenges the inference from intelligence or autonomous behavior to moral patienthood; it does not demonstrate that no possible future AI could have interests.

## Legal personhood: a divisible legal construction

**Joanna Bryson, Mihailis Diamantis, and Thomas Grant (2017) | [Of, for, and by the People](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10506-017-9214-9)**

- **In-principle possibility:** Legal personality need not track consciousness. Corporations and other nonhuman entities can have legally specified rights and obligations, and legal personhood is not all-or-nothing.
- **Objection to current AI personhood:** The authors argue that electronic personhood may shield developers, operators, or owners from accountability, while the moral interests it would protect remain uncertain.
- **Condition:** Specify the exact legal powers and duties, practical enforcement, remedies, and the parties ultimately responsible for harm.

**Claudio Novelli, Luciano Floridi, Giovanni Sartor, and Gunther Teubner (2025) | [AI as Legal Persons: Past, Patterns, and Prospects](https://doi.org/10.1111/jols.70021)**

- **Argument:** Shifts in legal-personhood debates depend on theories of legal personality, technical capacity, institutional integration, interactions among legal regimes, and precedent. The paper examines conditional pathways rather than an inevitable path to AI legal personality.
