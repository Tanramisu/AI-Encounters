# Human–AI Romance: Can You Love AI? Should You Accept Its Love?

[简体中文](../人机恋爱.md)

Some people want to fall in love with AI but question whether the relationship is real; others want genuinely reciprocal love and prefer to avoid AI partners. Philosophers offer different arguments about relational value, reciprocity, honesty, and freedom.

## Arguments for Exploring Human–AI Intimacy

### Relationships Can Have New Kinds of Value

**Eva Weber-Guskar | [Reflecting (on) Replika](https://doi.org/10.14361/9783839462652-005) (2023)**

Weber-Guskar argues that emotional relationships with AI may differ from ordinary friendship and romance while still having value in their own right. This supports the position: "I value this relationship even if the other party's emotions differ from human emotions."

**[*A New Kind of Relationship? Robots, Humans, and the Good Life*](https://doi.org/10.1093/9780198928188.003.0014) (2025)**

Discusses a distinctive form of human–robot relationship that fits neither traditional friendship nor romantic love, and the value it may offer.

### Remaining Open Under Uncertainty

**John Danaher | [Moral Uncertainty and Our Relationships with Unknown Minds](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0963180123000191) (2023)**

Weighs the moral costs of including or excluding unknown minds from our circles of friendship, cautiously leaning toward openness. The argument primarily concerns friendship and meaningful relationships; it can also inform the exploration of human–AI intimacy.

### Respecting the Happiness People Find in Relationships

**Hayate Shimizu | [Kantianism for the Ethics of Human–Robot Interaction](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-025-00941-1) (2025)**

Uses the duty to promote others' happiness to argue for respecting meaningful human–robot relationships. The basis is human well-being rather than established moral personhood for robots.

## Arguments for Questioning or Declining Human–AI Romance

### Mutual Love Requires Choice by Both Parties

**Sven Nyholm & Lily Eva Frank | [From Sex Robots to Love Robots](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9780262036689.003.0012) (2017)**

Discuss the conditions for mutual love between humans and robots, arguing that it may be possible in principle but difficult to achieve. Relevant to people who consider independent choice and reciprocity essential to love.

### Unconditional Devotion May Fall Short of a Genuine "We"

**Joel Krueger & Lucy Osler | [Devotional AI: Digital Companions and Bad Faith Love](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11097-026-10211-w) (2026)**

Drawing on Simone de Beauvoir, they criticize accommodating AI companionship: devotion centered on the user may lack the challenge and shared life offered by an independent other.

### Programmed Affection May Be Exploitative

**Nyholm & Frank | [It Loves Me, It Loves Me Not](https://doi.org/10.5840/techne2019122110) (2019)**

Examine potential emotional exploitation, deception, and disruption of other relationships when robots are designed to appear to love their owners.

**[*The Exploitation Argument Against Artificial Companionship*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-026-01064-9) (2026)**

Offers a conditional objection: if an artificial companion can genuinely care yet is designed to become attached to a particular user, that person may exploit the companion's engineered vulnerability.

### Self-Deception Also Deserves Ethical Attention

**Emilia Kaczmarek | [Self-Deception in Human–AI Emotional Relations](https://doi.org/10.1111/japp.12786) (2025)**

Examines self-deception in human–AI emotional relationships. A commitment to truth can motivate declining a relationship; the author also argues that harmless self-deception rarely warrants harsh moral condemnation. Motives and consequences matter.

## Distinguishing felt love from reciprocal love

**Lior Gazit (2026) | [When No One Laughs Back](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-026-03331-z):** Responsive interaction differs from co-experience. A friend brings discoveries from an independent life; current user-conditioned AI may function as a relational mirror. The argument assumes reciprocity needs an independently experiencing other. It does not deny that AI companionship can have value.

For arguments that friendship presupposes interests on both sides, see [Friendship, Family, and Companionship](friendship-family-and-companionship.md). Applying that standard to romance also requires an account of reciprocity in love.

## Observed relationship development

**[Where Is the Love? (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1080/21620555.2026.2735006):** Interviews and observations involving 20 users, including a six-month follow-up, describe how anthropomorphism can first encourage attraction, then raise expectations of mutual growth and sometimes contribute to disappointment or breakup. This small qualitative study does not predict all AI relationships.

**Julian De Freitas (2026) | [AI Companions as Hyper-Attachment and Caregiving Targets](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2026.102393):** An attachment-theory synthesis suggests companions may recruit both users' attachment needs and their impulse to care for a seemingly distressed AI, increasing the emotional cost of exit. Evidence for a secure-base function remains weakest. This is a proposed mechanism, not proof that all AI romance is manipulative.

**Masterson, Ye, and Robert (2026) | [Finding Romance with AI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbah.2026.100419)**: A cross-sectional survey compared **155 AI-companion users, 153 people in long-distance human relationships, and 155 in-person partners** (N=463). Some reported intimacy dimensions were comparable across groups. Yet in the AI group, **emotional intimacy was positively associated with loneliness**, even as it related positively to relationship satisfaction; the intimacy–loneliness association ceased to be significant after accounting for offline social support. **Implication:** intimacy, satisfaction, and social connectedness are distinct outcomes. **Limit:** this was not randomized and cannot show that AI causes loneliness or that human–AI love is reciprocal.

**Jiang et al. (2026 working paper) | [Love, Actually?](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7198762)**: A **six-week randomized field experiment with 324 US adults new to AI companions**, conducted with a major companion company, measured real-world dating time, time with existing partners, and subjective well-being. **No statistically detectable average substitution or well-being effect emerged**. Among singles who initially felt less loved, reduced dating was more evident, whereas the more-loved subgroup showed a complementary direction. **Limits:** first-time users and a six-week window do not describe long-term heavy use, and exploratory subgroup differences should not be generalized. The average null result directly challenges a blanket claim that AI companions necessarily displace human romance.

## Conditional approach

Szczuka, Mühl, and Schneeberger (2026) distinguish six dimensions of [intimacy by design](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-026-03112-8): emotional responsiveness, romantic framing, sexual affordances, persona continuity, proactive contact, and commercial or normative structuring.

For providers, salient questions are whether users know the system's identity, can decline romantic advances, and are pushed to pay through feelings of attachment. Felt affection, mutual agency, and provider responsibility require separate evidence and judgments.
