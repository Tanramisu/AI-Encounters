# Elder, Patient, and Disability Care: Assistance or Replacement of Human Care?

[简体中文](../老年病患与残障照护.md)

**Dilemma:** When institutions use robots for companionship, guidance, and physical assistance, do they enhance independent living or remove meaningful human contact in the name of efficiency? The preferences of older or disabled people cannot simply be replaced with those of families or providers.

## For targeted assistance

**Kim and Jeon (2026) | [Care Robots as Emerging Health Technologies](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e95232)**: A systematic review included 34 randomized studies with 2,476 participants across 17 countries; 20 entered meta-analysis. Under Hartung–Knapp small-sample corrections, **only 1 of 7 prespecified outcomes was statistically significant** (neuropsychiatric symptoms, Hedges g=0.44, 95% CI 0.03–0.84), and significance disappeared when one study with ambiguous data interpretation was removed. The other six domains were nonsignificant; certainty was low or very low and every trial raised risk-of-bias concerns. **For:** facilitator-supported adjunctive use may still help in selected cases. **Against wholesale substitution:** the evidence does not yet justify routine clinical adoption.

**Mihailov and Wangmo (2025) | [Does Humanness Matter?](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11948-025-00547-y)**: Qualitative interviews with Swiss older adults, family caregivers, and care professionals suggest that robots may preserve dignity in some intimate assistance and hygiene tasks. **Condition:** ask the recipient what forms of help feel dignified. **Limit:** interviews about preferences are not equivalent to comparative clinical outcomes.

## Against replacing human relationships

**Mihailov and Wangmo (2025) | same study:** Human care includes recognition, interpretation, and reciprocal social presence beyond task completion. Full replacement may remove these goods. **Objection:** a human-versus-machine dichotomy misses situations where someone prefers more privacy with a robot.

**[Ethical Considerations in the Use of Social Robots for Supporting Mental Health… (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11994439/)** identifies access inequity, consent, substitution of human care, and infantilization in long-term care. **Limit:** ethical case analysis does not establish that robotic assistance necessarily increases isolation.

## Consent by care task

Consent separately to task assistance, emotional companionship, monitoring, and physical restraint. Cognitive decline does not erase all decision-making capacity in every domain. Robotics may expand autonomy while also enabling surveillance or institutional neglect; compare actual user preferences, available alternatives, and measurable outcomes rather than deciding by whether the robot resembles a human.
