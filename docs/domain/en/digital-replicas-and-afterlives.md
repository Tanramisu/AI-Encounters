# Digital Replicas and Afterlives: May We Make the Dead Speak Again?

[简体中文](../真人替身与数字亡者.md)

**Dilemma:** Can reconstructing a person's voice, memories, and conversational style help survivors mourn? Who authorizes what a replica may say, its commercial use, and its termination?

## For continued remembrance

**Stephen M. Campbell, Pengbo Liu, and Sven Nyholm (2025) | [Can Chatbots Preserve Our Relationships with the Dead?](https://doi.org/10.1017/apa.2025.1)** argue that a thanabot relationship **cannot literally continue the prior relationship with the deceased**, since the bot is not the same individual, though its use may help a survivor sustain a continuing *bond with the person who died*. They also argue that a thanabot relationship **cannot equal the intrinsic value of a rewarding human intimate relationship**, while it may provide instrumental benefits. **Limits:** these are metaphysical and ethical arguments, not clinical findings that all memorial chatbots are harmful.

**[On Grief and Griefbots (2024)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/think/article/on-grief-and-griefbots/56DF2D4A7195C96209F35EC99671A755)** explores how conversational replicas might support or impede grieving. **Limit:** normative and conceptual reasoning, not trial evidence of therapeutic benefit.

## Against: the deceased and the living may lose control

**Hollanek and Nowaczyk-Basińska (2024) | [Griefbots, Deadbots, Postmortem Avatars](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-024-00744-w)**: Three speculative design scenarios distinguish the interests of deceased data donors, those holding their data, and people interacting with the replica. Unauthorized reconstruction, unsolicited contact, and monetization raise different problems. **Reasoning:** access to records is not equivalent to prior consent; even relatives can disagree about what mourning requires. **Limit:** design scenarios do not measure population harm rates.

**[Synthetic Afterlives: Deathbots as Affective Infrastructures of Memory (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1017/mem.2025.10013)** analyzes how commercial afterlife platforms turn complex memories into interactive products. Its criticism of commercialization does not show that every transparent, consented memorial interaction is wrong.

## Authorization and survivors' privacy

Distinguish **prior authorization, data inheritance, third-party privacy, living users' agency, and revocable access**. A model saying “I forgive you” is not proof of the deceased person's posthumous wishes. Account inheritance is not automatic moral authority to reconstruct someone's romantic promises. Simulating the younger identity of a person living with dementia must not eclipse that person's present dignity and choices.
