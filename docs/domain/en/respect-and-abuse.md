# Respect and Abuse: Why Might Mistreating AI Be Wrong?

[简体中文](../尊重与虐待.md)

Ethical objections to abusing AI may concern AI's own interests, human character, or social relationships.

## Human Moral Reactions

- **Bartneck & Keijsers (2020) | [The Morality of Abusing a Robot](https://doi.org/10.1515/pjbr-2020-0017)**: Participants watched similar abusive acts directed at a human or a computer-generated robot. Moral judgments did not differ significantly, but a robot's retaliation was judged more negatively.
- **Archer, Wilks & Sommer (2025) | [Evaluating the Morality of Violence Against Robots](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-24875-y)**: In a study with 297 participants watching NAO and Roomba robots being shut down, insulted, abused, or excluded, humanlike appearance increased moral concern in shutdown and exclusion scenarios. Robot protests had no significant overall effect.

Both experiments measured people's moral judgments.

**de Ruiter (2026) | [Reactive Attitudes in Human-AI Encounters](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-026-01223-0)**: Interviews with 11 AI-companion users recorded love, guilt, gratitude, resentment, and apologies for giving commands or crossing perceived boundaries.

## Why mistreatment might be wrong without felt AI suffering

**Mark Coeckelbergh (2021) | [Should We Treat Teddy Bear 2.0 as a Kantian Dog?](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11023-020-09554-3)**

- **Case for indirect moral consideration:** Even a robot lacking direct moral patienthood may merit respectful treatment because of human character, the feelings of other people, ongoing joint activity, or genuine uncertainty about its moral status. Coeckelbergh offers four sufficient, not necessary, grounds.
- **Conditions:** The encounter must actually involve these human interests, relationships, or uncertainties.
- **Objection:** Reasons ultimately grounded in human interests do not themselves establish that the robot suffers, holds equal rights, or must always be protected from damage.

**Mark Coeckelbergh (2021) | [How to Use Virtue Ethics for Thinking About the Moral Standing of Social Robots](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12369-020-00707-z)**

- **Practice-based argument:** Assess kicking robots or degrading anthropomorphic companions in light of cultivated habits, social history, and surrounding practices, not only a user's momentary intention.
- **Objection:** Claims that such acts cause real-world harm to humans still require independent causal evidence. Their moral meaning can vary across contexts.

## The strongest objection: revulsion is not legal justification

**Kate Darling (2016) | [Extending Legal Protection to Social Robots](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2044797)**

- **Argument for considering some protections:** People form genuine attachments to lifelike robots. Public mistreatment can distress observers and may affect habits of empathy; Darling explores legal protection beyond property law.
- **Condition:** The relevant harm to humans or society must warrant a proportionate intervention.
- **Objection:** Anthropomorphic reactions and disgust alone do not establish that mistreating robots causes mistreatment of humans.

**Paula Sweeney (2022) | [Why Indirect Harms Do Not Support Social Robot Rights](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11023-022-09593-y)**

- **Skeptical argument:** Reviewing proposals about bad character, desensitization, and rehearsal for violence, Sweeney finds insufficient persuasive evidence for legal rights or broad bans based on indirect harms. Restrictions might also undermine useful social-care robotics.
- **Conditions:** Her argument concerns robots not established as direct moral patients and laws justified by purported spillover harms; it does not settle the status of any future sentient AI.
- **Limit:** Weak evidence for general bans does not make every action permissible. Property damage or harm to users and bystanders can be assessed independently.

**Luck, Montefiore, and Bartel (2025) | [The Robo-Barbie Dilemma](https://doi.org/10.1093/pq/pqaf048):** Through a thought experiment about machines that convincingly resemble moral patients but are stipulated not to be such patients, the authors compare explanations based on character, social relations, and the gravity of simulated wrongdoing. This is normative analysis, not proof that robots suffer.

**Distinct judgments:** Direct AI welfare, harm to people, character and social practices, and legal restrictions each require their own grounds.
