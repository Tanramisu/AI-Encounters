# AI Coworkers and Co-Creation: Who Deserves Trust, Credit, and Recognition?

[简体中文](../AI同事与共同创作.md)

**Dilemma:** After collaborating with AI over time, should we treat it as a teammate? Who deserves attribution, payment, and recognition: users, prompt writers, developers, training-data contributors, or AI?

## For accounting for different contributions

**Jessica He, Stephanie Houde, and Justin D. Weisz, [Which Contributions Deserve Credit? (CHI 2025)](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18357v1)**: In a scenario-based survey of employees at one multinational technology company, 155 responses remained from 184 after screening for generative-AI experience and response quality. Participants were randomly assigned a human or AI writing partner and one of three writing contexts, then rated deserved authorship credit across contribution types, amounts, and initiative. Credit judgments varied by contribution, and AI partners generally received less credit than human partners for equivalent contributions. Participants also considered disclosure of AI involvement important and weighed contribution quality, personal values, and technology-related factors in their credit judgments. **Implication:** both the nature of a contribution and the partner's identity shape credit judgments; a binary AI-use label misses these distinctions. **Limit:** the sample came from one technology company and excluded respondents without generative-AI experience; these are judgments about hypothetical writing scenarios, not observed attribution behavior, copyright entitlements, or AI moral rights.

**Donal Khosrowi, Finola Finn, and Elinor Clark, [Engaging the Many-Hands Problem of Generative-AI Outputs (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43681-024-00440-7)** propose collective-centered creation (CCC), allocating creative credit according to relevance and non-redundancy, control, originality, effort, leadership and independence, and directness. Training-data creators, users, models, and developers may each contribute; developers' influence on general system capabilities is typically less direct than their contribution to a particular work. **Key objection:** weighting these criteria and establishing counterfactual contributions is difficult; the normative framework offers no formula for distributing copyright or compensation.

## Attribution and accountability

**ACM, [authorship and AI-use policy (2026)](https://2027.sigcse-ts.acm.org/info/policies-ai)**: Academic authorship carries responsibility for the correctness and integrity of the work. ACM requires methodological disclosure when AI is used in research activities directly relevant to its conclusions, but no longer requires disclosure of AI assistance used only in writing a submission; named authors remain accountable for its contents. **Rationale:** in publishing systems that require accountability, recording contributions and assigning authorship serve distinct purposes. **Key objection:** a publisher's accountability rules allocate responsibility for papers; they do not settle what professional recognition AI contributions deserve or whether AI has moral interests of its own.

## Empirical evidence on trust in AI teammates

**Wen Duan et al., [Trusting Autonomous Teammates in Human–AI Teams (CHI 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713527)**: This systematic review examined 57 empirical studies of human trust in autonomous AI teammates published from 2008 to 2022. Fourteen papers provided sufficient statistics to aggregate 18 effect sizes for eight agent-related factors. Reliability and transparency both influenced trust, although transparency had a smaller pooled effect and studies operationalized “trust” inconsistently. Of the 57 studies, 33 used military task settings and 44 studied two-member teams of one human and one AI; physically embodied robots were excluded. **Implication:** trust in collaboration has observable task and interaction determinants; whether AI has interests or independent legal responsibility requires separate arguments. **Limit:** the review measures human trust in AI, with concentrated task and team settings, rather than long-term creative partnerships.

## Contribution, credit, and interests

Distinguish **factual contribution**, **professional recognition**, **enforceable legal rights**, and **possible AI moral interests**. Fair credit does not require listing every model in every work, and “AI colleague” rhetoric must not hide the labor of people involved.
